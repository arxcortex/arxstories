# Brazil Votes in Runoff Between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro

*Final polls put the incumbent and the senator Trump has praised within the margin of error.*

![ΛRX Intercept dispatch card (illustration): Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro](card.png)

Brazilians voted Sunday in a presidential runoff between President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80, of the Workers' Party, and Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, of the Liberal Party. Voting is compulsory for literate citizens aged 18 to 70, and more than 158 million people are registered. Polls were due to close at 5 p.m. Brasília time; The Rio Times reports that a Supreme Court order kept them open until 7 p.m., and CNN also reported the extension.

Flávio led the Oct. 4 first round with 47% to Lula's 45%, with abstention at 21%, the highest first-round rate in 28 years, according to the Associated Press. Final polls compiled by The Rio Times mostly showed Flávio ahead by one to four points, within each survey's margin of error. Datafolha's last poll had the two tied.

Flávio is the son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a sentence of more than 27 years after the Supreme Court convicted him over a plot to overturn the 2022 election, which Lula won by under two points. Jair Bolsonaro denies the charges. Flávio has promised to pardon his father.

The United States hangs over the race. The Trump administration has placed an extra 25% tariff on most Brazilian goods, sanctioned the Supreme Court justice who led the Bolsonaro investigation, and designated two Brazilian gangs as terrorist organizations, a step Lula opposes and Flávio supports. President Trump called Flávio's first-round lead "a big victory."

Lula's campaign casts the vote as a stand against U.S. interference; Workers' Party chair Edinho Silva calls Flávio "dangerous." Lula promises to "do double of what we have done," pointing to programs such as Bolsa Família. Flávio says "Brazil needs change," and promises a crackdown on organized crime, spending cuts, and better deals with Washington, including on rare minerals. Each side faces corruption questions: Flávio has acknowledged taking millions from banker Daniel Vorcaro to fund a film about his father, and denies wrongdoing; Lula served more than a year in prison on corruption charges before his convictions were annulled.

One camp speaks of "interference," the other of "change"; beneath both is a contest over the presidency, over the courts that convicted a Bolsonaro, and over whether Brazil leans toward Washington or keeps its ties to Beijing.
