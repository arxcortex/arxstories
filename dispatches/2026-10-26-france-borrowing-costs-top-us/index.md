# France's Borrowing Costs Top America's as Prefects Ban Marches

*France's 10-year yield broke 5% for the first time since 2002 as the budget returns to the National Assembly on 27 October.*

Last week France paid more to borrow than America, for the first time since 2013.

Its 10-year yield broke 5%. Prefects banned marches from Toulon to Orléans.

The budget returns to the Assembly on Tuesday.

The government may skip the vote.

![ΛRX Intercept dispatch card (illustration): police barriers before a colonnaded Paris facade at dusk](card.png)

The yield on France's 10-year government bond, the OAT, crossed 5% on 21 October for the first time since 2002 and rose above the 10-year US Treasury yield for the first time since 2013, The Guardian reported. It reached 5.13% on 22 October, according to Banque de France data, and the spread over German 10-year Bunds hit about 175 basis points, the widest since November 2011. On Friday Moody's kept France at Aa3 with a negative outlook, and the yield eased back to about 4.93% on Monday, still about 156 basis points over Bunds. France ran a deficit of 5.1% of GDP in 2025 and carries debt of 115.6% of GDP. Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid says the selling has spread to Italian and Spanish bonds.

Protests over understaffed, crumbling schools and the cost of living turned violent on 17 October in Paris, Bordeaux, Nice, Marseille and Toulon; the interior ministry reported nearly 2,400 arrests. Ahead of the 24–25 October marches, prefects banned protests in parts of Toulon, Montauban, Saint-Étienne, Roanne, Lyon and Orléans, and an Orléans court upheld that city's ban. The interior ministry counted 410,000 marchers nationwide that day; the CGT union counted about 800,000. Unions have called a national strike day for 5 November. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu says he will detail a plan on teacher numbers, class sizes, buildings and Parcoursup this week.

The National Assembly resumes debate on the 2027 budget's tax measures on 27 October. The government aims to cut the deficit to 5% of GDP in 2027 from a projected 5.4% this year, with about €54 billion in savings. Without a majority, it can pass the bill under Article 49.3, without a final vote unless a no-confidence motion succeeds, or by ordinance if Parliament misses its 70-day deadline. Lecornu used 49.3 three times in January to pass the 2026 budget. The European Central Bank, which raised its key rate to 2.5% in September, decides on rates on Thursday, 29 October.

Economy minister Roland Lescure says France "will have a budget" because it has "two safety rails: 49.3 and ordinances," and calls that "not a show of force, but a passage through reason." CGT leader Sophie Binet says the protest bans mark "a worrying turn" and that "if the government wants to cancel the marches this weekend, it has the solution: respond immediately to the demands." Budget rapporteur Philippe Juvin says France must "spend less, spend better and work more."

The government says spending cuts will calm bondholders and protect France's credit; unions and students say the cuts fall on schools, wages and public services. Beneath both is a question of power: who pays down France's deficit, and whether Parliament or the executive decides.
